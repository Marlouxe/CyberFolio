Every year, millions of accounts get hacked because of a single problem: weak passwords. This guide explains everything, without jargon and without the headache.

80% of breaches involve a weak or stolen password
#1 most used password in the world for years
3s are enough to crack "123456" with automated software

Let's start with a simple observation

You may think it only happens to other people. But if your password looks like "mypassword", "qwerty", or your first name followed by your birth year... you're an easy target. Not because hackers are targeting you personally, but because automated programs test millions of combinations per second, around the clock, on every accessible account.

It's not a matter of bad luck. It's a matter of habits. And good habits can be learned in 5 minutes.

!

A single weak password can compromise all your accounts if you reuse it everywhere.

How does a password attack work?

No need to be a computer genius. Here's how it really works.

01

Brute force attack

A program tries every possible combination, thousands per second. A 6-digit password is cracked in a few seconds. 8 mixed characters: a few hours. 14 characters? Billions of years.

02

Dictionary attack

The "dictionary" is a list of millions of known passwords, collected from previous data leaks. "Sunshine2024", "Football75"... anything that looks like a real word is in there.

03

Massive data leaks

When a site gets hacked, its users' passwords end up for sale on the dark web. If you reuse your password everywhere, a single leak is enough to compromise all your accounts in a chain reaction.

The 4 golden rules of a good password

01

Long

At least 12 characters, ideally 16 or more. Each extra character exponentially multiplies the cracking time. Length is your best defense.

02

Mixed

Uppercase, lowercase, numbers and symbols (!@#$%...). A password combining all 4 types is much harder to crack than any combination using only 2.

03

Unique

A different password for every site and app. If one is compromised in a data leak, the others stay protected. One password for everything = a ticking time bomb.

04

Anonymous

Zero personal information: no name, first name, birth date, city, nickname, favorite football team. This info is often available on your social media and easy to guess.

The solution: password managers

Remembering dozens of long, complex passwords is humanly impossible. That's why password managers exist: apps that remember everything for you, encrypt your data and fill it in automatically. You only have one master password left to remember.

Bitwarden Free

Open source and publicly audited. The most transparent on the market. Ideal to get started, with a very complete free version. Available on all devices.

✓ Open source ✓ Cross-platform ✓ Free version
1Password Paid

Considered one of the most secure. Very polished interface, ideal for families (password sharing between members). Around €3/month.

✓ Polished interface ✓ Family sharing ✓ Very secure
Dashlane Free + Pro

Functional free version to get started. The paid version includes a dark web monitor that alerts you if your data has leaked somewhere.

✓ Dark web monitor ✓ Easy to use ✓ Free version
KeePass Free

Your passwords are stored locally on your device, not in a cloud. Ideal if you don't trust any online service. More technical but very robust.

✓ 100% local ✓ No cloud ✓ Free and open source

Browsers also offer a built-in manager (Chrome, Firefox, Safari). It's better than nothing, but a dedicated manager is still safer: it works across all browsers and apps, not just on the web.

🔐

Two-factor authentication (2FA)

Even with a perfect password, always enable two-factor authentication. It's an extra layer of security: even if your password is stolen, nobody can access your account without the second factor.

Enable it first on: your email, your social media, your online banking, and any site where important information is stored.

1
You enter your password As usual

Mistakes to never make again

✕
Using the same password everywhere

If a single site is hacked, all your accounts are exposed. It's the most important rule to follow.

✕
Storing your passwords in a text file, an email or a sticky note

These aren't secure. Use a dedicated password manager.

✕
Choosing personal information as a password

Your name, birth date, city, sports team... all of that is easy to guess, especially if you're active on social media.

✕
Giving your password by phone or email

No legitimate service will ever ask for your password. If someone does, it's a scam attempt.

✕
Staying logged in on a public or shared computer

Always log out after use in a library, an internet café, or at someone else's place.

✕
Ignoring data breach alerts

If a service warns you of a breach, immediately change your password on that site AND on all the others where you use it (yet another good reason to have unique passwords).
