Deepfakes

When you can no longer believe what you see and hear.

● REC
"Soldiers, lay down your arms."
AI-generated, never spoken

The word "deepfake" comes from deep learning and fake. It refers to a video, audio or image created or altered by an AI to make someone say or do something they never said or did.

The principle relies on neural networks: programs that learn by observing thousands of examples. You feed them photos, videos or recordings of a person, and they learn to reproduce their face, expressions and voice. Then you can paste that face onto another body or make that synthetic voice say any sentence.

Face swap

Replaces one person's face with another's in a video.

Voice cloning

Reproduces a voice from just a few seconds of recording.

Lip sync

Makes a filmed person say new text.

Full generation

Creates a person or a scene from scratch that doesn't exist.

What has changed: making a deepfake used to require skills and expensive equipment. Today, consumer apps do it in minutes, sometimes for free. A few seconds of a voice posted on social media can be enough to clone it.

What scammers do with them

The fake CEO

A scammer imitates an executive to push an employee into making an urgent transfer.

The fake relative

A cloned voice calls a parent: accident, money trouble.

Disinformation

Fake statements from political leaders.

Blackmail

Doctored images of real people, harassment.

Can you spot them?

Sometimes. In a video: blurry or wobbly face edges, blinking that's too rare or too regular, poorly defined teeth and hair, lighting that doesn't match the setting, lips slightly out of sync with the sound. For voice: monotone, no breathing, strange silences.

But let's be honest: these clues vanish as the technology improves. Even experts get fooled, and automatic detectors aren't 100% reliable. The right reflex isn't to play detective, but to change your method: stop judging a message by its appearance, and verify it through another channel.

These scams work because they exploit trust and emotion rather than technique. Scammers almost always add urgency, fear or secrecy ("don't tell anyone") to stop you from thinking.

Very real stories

  • 2018Education

    Jordan Peele and BuzzFeed make Barack Obama "speak" to warn about the danger of deepfakes.

  • 2019Voice cloning

    The head of a British energy company believes he's speaking to the boss of his German parent company and wires about €220,000 to scammers. One of the first known cases of cloned-voice fraud.

  • 2021Entertainment

    Fake "Tom Cruise" clips get millions of views on TikTok: an impersonator whose face is retouched by a specialized artist.

  • 2022Disinformation

    A fake video of Volodymyr Zelensky calling on his soldiers to lay down their arms circulates after a news website is hacked. Crude, it is quickly debunked.

  • 2023Viral

    Pope Francis in a white puffer jacket, an AI-generated image, fools millions of internet users.

  • 2024Video call

    At Arup in Hong Kong, an employee joins a video call with his chief financial officer and colleagues, all deepfakes. He wires about 25 million dollars. January 2024: a fake Joe Biden voice calls New Hampshire voters telling them not to vote; fake images of Taylor Swift are viewed tens of millions of times.

  • 2025In France

    The "fake Brad Pitt": a woman, convinced for over a year that she was in a relationship with the actor, pays around €830,000. She has spoken out publicly to warn other victims.

It's not all dark: deepfakes are also used in film, advertising and education.

What the law says, and where to get help

In France, creating and sharing an AI-made edit without the consent of the person depicted is punishable by one year in prison and a €15,000 fine, with heavier penalties online. You can report content on Pharos and look for help on cybermalveillance.gouv.fr.

Key takeawayAn image, a video or a voice is no longer enough to prove anything. Take time to doubt, verify through another channel, and never let yourself be rushed.
