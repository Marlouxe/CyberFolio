Quishing

When a simple QR code becomes a trap.

Where does this square lead? Impossible to read…

The word "quishing" is a blend of QR code and phishing. A cybercriminal hides a malicious link behind a QR code and hopes you'll scan it with your phone. You then land on a fake site imitating a bank, a government agency or a payment service, and you're asked for your credentials or your bank card.

A QR code isn't dangerous in itself. Invented in 1994 by the Japanese company Denso Wave to track parts in car factories, it's a two-dimensional barcode containing text, most often a web address. The problem: nobody can read that content with the naked eye. There's no way to guess whether a black and white square leads to a legitimate site or to a scam.

Why it works: with the health crisis (restaurant menus, certificates, contactless payments), we got used to scanning without thinking. Criminals understood that very well.

Why scammers love it

It's an image

Spam filters mostly analyze the text and links of emails, not images.

Your personal phone

You scan with a smartphone that is often less protected than a work computer.

A hidden link

You can't hover over the link to check the address before opening it.

An editable address

A dynamic QR code can lead to a harmless page, then to a fake site.

Where is the trap hiding?

The parking meter

A sticker placed over the real QR code: you think you're paying for parking, you're handing over your card.

The fake fine

A fake ticket under the windshield wiper or in your mailbox.

The "boss" email

A message asking you to scan a code to "reactivate" your work access.

The fake buyer

On classified ads, a QR code "to receive your payment" that actually makes you send one.

Charging stations

Fake codes stuck on electric car chargers, flyers or menus.

Good news: scanning a QR code doesn't hack a phone by itself. The danger starts when you open the link, enter information or install something. So there's always a chance to stop. The scammer, for their part, creates urgency: "parking expired", "parcel blocked", "account suspended".

My reflexes before scanning

  1. Wasn't I expecting this code? I don't scan it. A code received by mail, by email or found in the street is treated like a suspicious link.
  2. I read the address. The camera displays it before opening: weird domain name, typos, shortened link or an address unrelated to the stated organization = alarm.
  3. I check the surface. Sticker sticking out, peeling off or covering another one: I don't scan.
  4. I use the official door. For a bank, taxes or health insurance, I type the address myself or open my usual app.
  5. I enter nothing unexpected. No password or bank card on a page that showed up by surprise. At work, I report an email containing only a QR code to the IT department.
Too late? Quickly change the affected password, block your card with your bank, monitor your accounts, then report the scam. For card fraud, the Perceval platform exists in France; cybermalveillance.gouv.fr can support you; file a complaint if you've suffered harm.

Very real stories

  • 1994Invention

    Denso Wave creates the QR code to track parts in Japanese car factories.

  • 2020Boom

    Restaurant menus, certificates, payments: scanning becomes a daily reflex.

  • January 2022Alert

    The FBI issues a warning: criminals are tampering with QR codes to divert payments and steal credentials.

  • February 2022Super Bowl

    A Coinbase ad shows only a QR code bouncing around the screen, like the old DVD player logo. Millions of people scan it and the site crashes. The ad is legitimate, but it proves we scan without knowing where it leads.

  • 2022Texas

    Austin, San Antonio and other cities warn their residents: fake QR codes have been stuck on parking meters.

  • Since 2023In France

    Warnings relayed by municipalities and the press about fake codes on parking meters and fake fine notices; security vendors are seeing a sharp rise in quishing by email.

Key takeawayThe QR code isn't an enemy, it's a handy tool. Treat it like any link: where does it come from, why am I being asked to scan it, and do I really need it?
